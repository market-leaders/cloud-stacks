The Problem: Why "Handshake Deals" Don't Work in Louisiana Real Estate

Failing to legally define ownership at purchase is the biggest mistake unmarried homebuyers make.

Without the framework of marriage, every aspect of co-ownership is up for debate unless it's put in writing. The default rules in Louisiana can lead to outcomes that feel deeply unfair, especially if one partner contributed significantly more financially. A verbal agreement about who paid for the new roof or the kitchen remodel won't hold up in court.

The Myth of "Common Law Marriage" in Louisiana

Let's clear this up immediately: Louisiana does not recognize common-law marriage. It doesn't matter if you've lived together for 20 years and share a last name; you do not gain the same property rights as a married couple. Without a marriage certificate, the legal system views you as two unrelated individuals who happen to co-own a piece of property, much like business partners.

The Default Dilemma: Co-Ownership "In Indivision"

The default legal status for unmarried co-owners in Louisiana is "owners in indivision," which is similar to what other states call "tenants in common." The major pitfall here is that the law presumes a 50/50 split of ownership. This presumption applies regardless of who paid the down payment, who makes the monthly mortgage payments, or who funded the renovations.

Imagine this scenario: Partner A puts down 80% of the down payment on a home in Mandeville, and Partner B contributes 20%. If they break up and the house is sold, Partner B is legally entitled to 50% of the proceeds unless a prior written agreement states otherwise. That's a tough pill to swallow.

"He Said, She Said": Proving Financial Contributions After the Fact

Proving unequal financial contributions in court without a written agreement is an uphill battle. While you can present receipts, bank statements, and canceled checks, this evidence can be messy and may not be enough to overcome the strong legal presumption of equal ownership. This is where disputes get expensive, public, and incredibly bitter.

The Proactive Solution: Building a Strong Legal Foundation Before You Buy

A well-drafted co-ownership agreement is the single most effective tool for protecting both partners' interests.

Think of this agreement as a business plan for your shared property. It’s not about planning for a breakup; it’s about creating clarity and fairness for everyone involved, ensuring your investment is secure no matter what the future holds. Having these conversations upfront prevents misunderstandings down the road.

Step 1: Taking Title the Right Way

As "owners in indivision," you can and should specify ownership percentages on the Act of Sale (the legal document transferring the deed vs title). This is a crucial first step. For example, the document could state: "Jane Doe, an undivided sixty percent (60%) interest, and John Smith, an undivided forty percent (40%) interest." However, this only addresses the initial ownership split and doesn't cover ongoing expenses or an exit strategy, which is why the next step is essential.

Step 2: Crafting Your Real Estate "Prenup"

A Co-Ownership Agreement is a practical, necessary document that acts as a "house prenup." It provides a clear roadmap for your shared investment. Framing it as a way to have important conversations *before* a conflict arises can remove the emotional weight and focus on the practicalities of a major financial partnership.

Key Clauses Every Louisiana Co-Ownership Agreement Should Include

  • Initial Contributions: Clearly state the exact dollar amount each partner contributed to the down payment and closing costs.
  • Ongoing Expenses: Define who pays what percentage of the mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and any HOA fees. Will it be 50/50, or proportional to your incomes or ownership stake?
  • Repairs & Renovations: Create a system for approving and paying for major repairs or improvements. For example, any expense over $1,000 requires written consent from both partners.
  • Exit Strategy: Detail the process if the relationship ends or one partner wants to sell (the "3 D's": Death, Disability, or Disagreement). This section is vital and should include buyout clauses, a right of first refusal, and a clear method for property appraisal to determine fair market value. This also ties into the importance of effective estate planning for each individual.

What Happens When There's No Agreement? The "Partition" Process

If co-owners cannot agree on what to do with a property, any owner can file a "partition lawsuit" to force a resolution. There are two types: "partition in kind," where the property is physically divided (extremely rare for a single-family home), and "partition by licitation," which forces a sale of the property, often at a public sheriff's auction. This is a costly, time-consuming, and public process that a co-ownership agreement is designed to avoid.
