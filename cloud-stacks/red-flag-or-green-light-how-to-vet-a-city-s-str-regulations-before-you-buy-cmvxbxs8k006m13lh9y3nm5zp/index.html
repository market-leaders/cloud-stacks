Red Flag or Green Light? How to Vet a City’s STR Regulations Before You Buy

Before buying a short-term rental property, you must research the city's specific ordinances, zoning laws, and tax requirements, and look for signs of future regulatory risk like license caps, moratoriums, or active neighborhood opposition. Buying a property for a short-term rental is one of the biggest investments you can make. But in today's fast-changing regulatory landscape, a single city council vote can turn a profitable asset into a financial liability overnight. This guide is your pre-purchase playbook for analyzing a city's STR landscape, helping you invest with confidence.

Key Takeaways

  • Due Diligence is Non-Negotiable: Never assume a market is "STR-friendly" without verifying the specific municipal codes, zoning laws, and licensing requirements yourself.
  • Look for Future Risks, Not Just Current Rules: A city's current laws are only half the story. Investigate proposed ordinances, local news sentiment, and neighborhood group activity to spot future red flags.
  • Connect with Local Owners for Ground Truth: Official rules don't always reflect the reality of enforcement or neighborhood dynamics. Local owners offer invaluable on-the-ground intelligence.
  • Your Long-Term Success Depends on Staying Informed: The work doesn't stop after you buy. Ongoing monitoring of local regulations is critical to protecting your investment, which is where an owner-centric organization becomes essential.

The STR Vetting Playbook: From City Hall to Local Chatter

Where can I find a city's official STR rules?

Official STR rules are typically found on the city's official website, usually under the planning, zoning, code enforcement, or business licensing departments. This is the only source of truth; everything else is just commentary. Your first step is to go directly to the source and read the law for yourself.

Start with the City's Official Website

Most cities with established regulations have a dedicated section on their website. Use the site's search function for terms like "Short-Term Rentals," "Vacation Rentals," or "Lodging." Look for pages under departments like Planning & Zoning, Business Licensing, or Code Enforcement, as these are the agencies that typically manage STR permits and compliance.

Search the Municipal Code Library

If a simple search doesn't work, find the city's full municipal code. Services like Municode or Code Publishing often host these online libraries. Search within the code for the same keywords. Be prepared to read dense legal text, but this is where the actual, enforceable rules live—not in a blog post or a forum comment.

Make a Direct Call

When in doubt, call the city's planning or business licensing department. Be specific with your request: "I'm considering purchasing a property at [address or neighborhood] and would like to understand the complete requirements for operating a short-term rental." This direct approach can often clarify confusing language and uncover unwritten policies or pending changes.

What specific STR regulations should I look for?

You should look for specific regulations covering zoning restrictions, license or permit caps, primary residency requirements, and all applicable taxes. These four pillars will determine whether your investment is viable, profitable, or even legal. A misunderstanding in any of these areas can be a fatal flaw in your business plan.

Zoning & Permitted Areas

This is the most fundamental question: is the property you're considering in a zone where STRs are explicitly permitted? Some cities, like Sedona, Arizona, have complex zoning overlays that permit STRs in some areas but prohibit them in others. Check the city’s zoning map and cross-reference it with the STR ordinance to confirm the property is eligible. Don't take a seller's or real estate agent's word for it—verify it yourself.

License Caps & Waitlists

Many popular destinations have implemented caps on the number of STR licenses to manage tourism growth. A city might have a city-wide cap, or more granular caps by neighborhood or zone. Find out if a cap exists, if it has been reached, and if there is a waitlist. A multi-year waitlist makes a potential investment far more speculative.

Owner-Occupancy & Primary Residency Rules

This is a critical distinction. Does the ordinance require the owner to live on-site (owner-occupancy) or live in the property for a certain number of days per year (primary residency)? This rule separates a true investment property from a "house-hacking" opportunity. Cities like Denver have strict primary residence requirements, making non-local ownership for STR purposes impossible.

Taxes, Fees, and Insurance

Identify every cost associated with legal operation. This includes lodging taxes, occupancy taxes, tourism fees, and the annual cost of the license or permit itself. Additionally, check for specific liability insurance requirements. These costs must be factored into your financial projections to get a true picture of your potential return on investment.

What are the biggest red flags in a city's STR regulations?

The biggest red flags include outright bans, moratoriums on new licenses, non-transferable permits, and a history of frequent, restrictive rule changes. These signals point to an unstable and high-risk regulatory environment where your investment could be jeopardized with little warning. An owner-first approach, like the one fostered by ASTRO, helps you spot these risks early.

Moratoriums and Outright Bans

This is the most obvious red flag. An outright ban is a clear "no-go." A moratorium—a temporary pause on issuing new permits—is equally dangerous. It signals that the city council is actively considering more restrictive permanent rules, and the outcome is rarely favorable for owners.

Non-Transferable Licenses

Pay close attention to whether a license is tied to the owner or the property. If a license is non-transferable, you cannot simply "buy" a property that is already a successful STR and continue operating it. You will have to apply for a new license from scratch, and if the city has a license cap, you may not be able to get one. This kills the value of acquiring a turnkey rental business.

Vocal Anti-STR Community Groups

The legal code is only part of the story. Search local news archives and community forums (like Nextdoor or local Facebook groups) for the city's name + "short-term rental." A high volume of organized opposition, frequent negative news stories, or active anti-STR neighborhood groups are major indicators of future regulatory risk. These groups often have the ear of local politicians, making them a powerful force for restrictive changes.

A History of Sudden Changes

Review the city's legislative history. If a city has a track record of amending its STR rules every one or two years, it signals an unstable and unpredictable market. Investing in a market with regulatory whiplash is a gamble, as the rules you buy under may not be the rules you operate under a year later.

The Long-Term Strategy: Protecting Your Investment After You Buy

How can I predict future STR rule changes in a market?

You can predict future rule changes by consistently monitoring city council meeting agendas, tracking local news reports, and staying aware of neighborhood association discussions. This proactive monitoring is the only way to get ahead of proposals that could impact your business. However, doing this effectively is a massive commitment.

The Challenge of Constant Monitoring

Doing this research across one or more markets is time-consuming and complex. You have to know what to look for in dense legal documents and meeting minutes. The rules that decide whether you can keep renting get written in rooms you'll never have time to sit in. This is a significant burden for any independent owner.

Why should I connect with local STR owners before buying?

Connecting with local owners provides invaluable on-the-ground insight into enforcement realities, true operational costs, and neighborhood sentiment not found in official ordinances. These owners have firsthand experience and can give you a real-world perspective that documents alone cannot. They are your most valuable source of intelligence.

Getting the Real Story

Local hosts can tell you if a specific rule is strictly enforced or largely ignored. They can share tips on reliable cleaners, handymen, and how to navigate the local permit office efficiently. This "ground truth" can save you countless hours and thousands of dollars in mistakes.

The Power of a Network

A strong local owner network is your best defense against unfair regulations. When owners are coordinated, their collective voice is much more powerful in city hall. A single owner is a complaint; a group of organized owners is a constituency. This is the core of effective advocacy.
