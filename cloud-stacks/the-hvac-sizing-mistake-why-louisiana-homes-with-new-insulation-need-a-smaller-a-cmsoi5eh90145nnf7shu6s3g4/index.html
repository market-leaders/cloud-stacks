  • An oversized AC unit "short-cycles"—it cools the air too quickly to effectively remove humidity, leaving your home feeling cold and clammy.

The Problem: Why "Bigger is Better" is a Myth for Air Conditioners

An oversized HVAC system actually hurts your home's comfort and efficiency. An air conditioner's most critical job in a humid climate like New Orleans isn't just cooling the air; it's dehumidifying it. This process requires long, steady run times. When an AC unit is too powerful for a well-insulated space, it blasts the room with cold air and shuts off before it has a chance to pull the moisture out, creating a cycle of inefficiency and discomfort.

The "Short-Cycling" Trap: Why Your Home Feels Cold but Clammy

Short-cycling happens when the thermostat is satisfied by the temperature drop long before the evaporator coil has run long enough to condense and drain away humidity. This leads directly to that sticky, damp feeling you get in a Metairie home even when the thermostat reads 72 degrees. This lingering indoor humidity doesn't just feel bad; it can also promote the growth of mold and mildew.

Outdated Rules of Thumb vs. Modern Building Science

Many HVAC sizing estimates are still based on old "square feet per ton" rules. Let's be clear: these rules were created for old, leaky, uninsulated Louisiana homes. They are completely inaccurate for a home that has been air-sealed and insulated with modern materials like spray foam insulation. The building science expertise of Sunlight Contractors bridges the gap between creating a high-performance building envelope and ensuring your mechanical systems work with it, not against it.

The Hidden Costs: Higher Energy Bills and Premature HVAC Failure

Short-cycling is expensive. The compressor constantly starts and stops, which uses a surge of electricity each time and is the most stressful part of its operational cycle. Think of it like driving a car in stop-and-go city traffic versus smooth highway driving. The constant starting and stopping wears out the engine (or compressor) much faster and burns more fuel (electricity), leading to high energy bills and a shorter lifespan for your unit.

The Solution: How Modern Insulation Rewrites the Rules

High-performance insulation creates an airtight seal, drastically reducing your home's cooling load. When Sunlight Contractors installs spray foam or blown-in insulation, we aren't just adding a thermal blanket; we're creating a comprehensive air barrier. This barrier stops the relentless infiltration of hot, humid New Orleans air and prevents your conditioned air from escaping. Your home now requires significantly less energy to maintain its temperature, which is why your old HVAC sizing is now obsolete.

How Spray Foam and Blown-In Insulation Change the Game

Spray foam insulation acts as both an insulator and an air/moisture barrier, sealing the thousands of tiny cracks and gaps that old insulation couldn't. Blown-in insulation creates a seamless thermal cap in the attic, stopping the primary source of heat gain in Louisiana homes—the super-heated attic. Both of these solutions from Sunlight Contractors reduce the "tonnage" (cooling power) a home requires.

The Right-Sized AC Advantage: Superior Comfort and Dehumidification

A smaller, correctly-sized AC unit runs for longer, gentler cycles. This is the ideal scenario. It effectively pulls moisture from the air, creating a crisp, dry, comfortable feeling that an oversized unit can never achieve. This also means lower utility bills, less stress on components for a longer system lifespan, and quieter operation without the constant jarring on/off cycles.

The Key to Accurate Sizing: The Manual J Calculation

The industry-standard solution is the Manual J load calculation. This is not a guess or a rule of thumb. It is a comprehensive analysis that considers insulation levels, window efficiency, air leakage, home orientation, and local climate data to determine the precise cooling load of a house. This is the only correct way to size an HVAC system in a modern, well-insulated home.
