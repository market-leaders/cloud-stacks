Part 1: The Welcome Wagon - Your Harmless & Helpful Roommates

Most wildlife you'll encounter in your Cabo home are harmless, charming, and even helpful roommates who are simply part of the incredible local environment. This first section is designed to be reassuring and fun, addressing the most common, low-stakes encounters and framing them as a positive part of the Cabo experience.

The Gecko: Your In-House, All-Natural Pest Control

What they are: Small, chirping lizards often found on walls and ceilings, especially near lights at night.

They're the best roommates you could ask for. They eat bugs, don't leave dishes in the sink, and offer a friendly chirp to let you know they're on the job. That little sound is the victory cry of a mosquito being devoured before it can find you.

Geckos are completely harmless to humans and a sign of a healthy, low-pesticide environment. Don't spray them or shoo them out; celebrate them! They are nature's perfect, silent guardians against spiders, moths, and other insects.

The Iguana & Desert Spiny Lizard: The Sunbathing Lawn Ornaments

What they are: Larger, prehistoric-looking lizards you'll see sunning themselves on rocks, walls, and pool decks.

Think of them as living garden statues that occasionally do push-ups to impress each other. Seeing a large, majestic iguana perched on a warm wall is a quintessential part of the living in Los Cabos experience. They add a touch of the wild and exotic to your landscaping.

These lizards are herbivores and generally very shy. They won't bother you, but it's best not to try to pet them. If you have prize-winning hibiscus flowers, you might want to plant them in a protected courtyard, as iguanas find them to be a five-star delicacy.

The Birds of Paradise: From Hummingbirds to the Crested Caracara

What they are: A vibrant and noisy array of birdlife that provides a daily show.

Your morning coffee on the terrace will now have a soundtrack, courtesy of the local bird population. From the frantic energy of tiny hummingbirds darting between flowers to the comical gait of the roadrunner, the avian life is a constant source of entertainment. You'll quickly learn to identify different calls and songs.

Putting out a simple hummingbird feeder can bring a whirlwind of activity to your patio. Keep an eye out for the majestic Crested Caracara, often called the "Mexican Eagle," soaring overhead. Birdwatching is one of the many top things to do in Cabo that you can enjoy from the comfort of your own home.

Part 2: The Respectful Neighbors - Coexisting with Larger Baja Wildlife

Understanding and respecting larger Cabo wildlife is key to safely enjoying the region's more remote and beautiful properties, especially as you explore areas further from the city centers. It directly connects wildlife types to the specific geographic areas we serve, showcasing our deep local expertise.

The Coyote: The Smart and Shy Desert Dog

What they are: A common sight, especially in communities bordering the desert or on golf courses at dawn and dusk.

They're the original neighborhood watch, always aware of what's going on but preferring to keep their distance. Coyotes are intelligent, adaptable, and a natural part of the Baja ecosystem. Seeing one trot silently across an arroyo is a beautiful, wild moment.

Coyotes are naturally wary of humans. The key to keeping it that way is to never, ever feed them, either intentionally or unintentionally. This means using trash cans with secure lids and not leaving pet food outside. For pet owners, especially those with small dogs, it's wise to supervise them outside, particularly during the low-light hours of dawn and dusk.

The Snakes & Scorpions: A Healthy Dose of Awareness

What they are: The creatures that tend to cause the most anxiety for newcomers to desert living.

The best way to avoid a scorpion is to not walk around barefoot in the dark. Your feet will thank you. While it sounds intimidating, a little awareness goes a long way and quickly becomes second nature.

It's important to reassure newcomers that dangerous encounters are extremely rare. Simple habits, like shaking out shoes and clothes before putting them on, prevent most surprises. Many long-term residents buy a blacklight flashlight; scanning your floors and walls at night can be a fun, sci-fi activity rather than a scary chore. Most snakes you might see are non-venomous and beneficial for controlling rodents.
